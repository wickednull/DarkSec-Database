# Shelter & Fire

> **Core temperature is a life-support parameter.** Wind, wet clothing and ground contact drain heat far faster than most people expect. Shelter first, then fire, then water.

## Shelter selection
- Look up: dead branches, rockfall, avalanche runout, flood debris lines.
- Look down: drainage, insect nests, roots, unstable ground.
- Avoid ridge tops and lone trees in lightning; avoid dry creek beds in rain.
- Near water is convenient but colder, wetter and buggier. Camp above the high-water line.
- Mark your shelter so rescuers can see it. A bright tarp is a signal as well as a roof.

## Build order
1. **Weather protection:** tarp, poncho, bark, leaf litter. Windward wall first.
2. **Ground insulation:** at least 10 cm of dry leaves, pine boughs, cardboard or a sleeping pad. Conduction to cold ground is a major heat loss.
3. **Heat trap:** a small enclosed space warms faster than a large one. Keep a vent for air.
4. **Bed:** raise the sleeping area slightly; dig a shallow drainage channel around the shelter.
5. **Fire:** only after the shelter is secure and ventilation is planned.

## Shelter types
- **Tarp lean-to:** fast, versatile, good with a fire wall. Pitch the back to the wind.
- **A-frame:** ridge line between two anchors, tarp draped both sides, stakes/guys.
- **Debris hut:** thick leaf/branch insulation over a frame; excellent cold-weather insulation but takes work and is a fire risk.
- **Snow cave/trench:** insulate floor, keep a vent hole, avoid overheating. Only with adequate snow stability and knowledge.
- **Vehicle:** out of wind, but never run the engine in an enclosed space. Crack a window for ventilation; clear the exhaust.

## Fire fundamentals
**Heat + fuel + oxygen.** Prepare three sizes of fuel before you strike:
1. **Tinder:** dry grass, birch bark, fatwood shavings, char cloth, cotton + petroleum jelly.
2. **Kindling:** pencil-to-finger-sized dry twigs.
3. **Fuel:** wrist-sized and larger dead wood.

Start small. Protect the flame from wind and rain. Feed gradually. Have water/dirt ready. Never leave a fire unattended.

### Ignition methods
- Lighter (carry two, keep dry).
- Ferrocerium rod: scrape a pile of magnesium, strike sparks into it.
- Matches: waterproof, strike on the box; carry in a sealed bag.
- Battery + steel wool (emergency; can ignite violently).
- Magnifying lens in strong sun.
- Flint and steel with char cloth.

### Fire lays
- **Teepee:** quick light, good for boiling.
- **Log cabin:** stable cooking fire.
- **Dakota hole:** hidden, windy conditions.
- **Rocket stove:** efficient with small fuel; requires a proper stove or careful construction.
- **Long fire / reflector:** warmth for a shelter; build a reflector wall of green logs/rocks (avoid wet river rocks — they can explode).

## Carbon monoxide and fire safety
- **Never** burn charcoal indoors, in a tent, or in a vehicle.
- Generators and camp stoves produce CO. Use outdoors, away from windows and vents.
- Keep a CO alarm in any structure with combustion.
- Maintain 1 m clearance around stoves and heaters; keep children and sleeping bags away.
- Extinguish fires completely before sleeping or leaving. Drown, stir, drown.

## Cold-weather survival
- Dress in layers: base wicking, mid insulation, outer wind/rain. Avoid cotton next to skin.
- Protect extremities: hat, gloves, dry socks. Change socks before they freeze.
- Eat and drink; dehydration and low blood sugar accelerate hypothermia.
- Watch for the "umbles" — stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, grumbles — early hypothermia.
- Do not sleep directly on snow; insulate.

## Heat survival
- Shelter during the hottest hours. Move at dawn/dusk.
- Wet clothing and skin, fan, use shade and evaporation.
- Drink before thirst; add salt/electrolytes if sweating heavily.
- Never leave children or pets in a vehicle.
